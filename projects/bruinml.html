The research question

A small unfamiliar region can disappear when an image is summarized as a whole. Our five-person BruinML team studied training-free detection of these regions using frozen CLIP patch features.

My experiments

I designed three Python Monte Carlo studies. The initial sweep covered 225 configurations: five noise distributions, five object geometries, and nine signal strengths. I compared global, maximum, top-k, and spatial-scan aggregation.

I kept fitting, calibration, and evaluation samples separate, recorded the configurations and results, and used Wilson intervals and paired bootstrap comparisons to examine uncertainty.

A clipped spatial scan

I implemented clipping and null-centering of patch scores to limit the effect of extreme values. In one Gaussian-mixture simulation, the clipped scan increased detection power from 19.9% to 97.7% relative to the original unclipped scan, at a 5% false-positive target.

That is a controlled simulation result for a particular setting, not an overall accuracy score or a measured improvement on every real-image dataset.

Geometry and dispersion

Fixed-size geometry and fixed-total-signal experiments helped isolate where aggregation fails. Spreading the same total signal across an image can make a scan less effective; it is a diagnostic finding rather than an improvement claim.

The manuscript

I produced three figures and reproducible experiment records and contributed experiments, analysis, and writing to the coauthored manuscript. We submitted it to Algorithmic Learning Theory (ALT 2027); it remains under review.

The CLIP project is completed. My ongoing BruinML work on demonstration ranking is a separate project.
